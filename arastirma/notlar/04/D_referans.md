## 1. Important corrections to the brief

1. Scottish Highlands and Scandes are NOT intermediate (60 to 300 Ma). Their last major orogenic deformation is Silurian to Devonian (about 425 to 390 Ma), so they belong in the old class. Their present relief is partly Cenozoic uplift (see section 4).
2. Cape Fold Belt is NOT old (over 300 Ma). Its final deformation phase is about 253 to 249 Ma (Gondwana Research 2019, search summary), so it belongs in the intermediate class by the brief's own bins.
3. Urals final compression ended in the Early Triassic (about 245 Ma), so it is intermediate, not old; its main phase is Permian (299 to 251 Ma) (Britannica, search summary).
4. Atlas: the High Atlas is Cenozoic (Alpine) uplift, so it belongs to the young class; only the Anti-Atlas is Paleozoic (Wiki). Listed as young in the table with a note.
5. Pyrenees last compression is early Oligocene to early Miocene, so it is young (under 60 Ma) as the brief expects.
6. Appalachian relief: the "300 to 600 m" figure in the brief is NOT in the USGS source. The USGS says Valley and Ridge local relief is about 1000 ft (about 300 m) and Plateau relief about 500 ft (about 150 m). This is the only survey-level figure found for the Appalachians.
7. Land-area percentages corrected: GMBA mountains are 12.3% of land outside Antarctica (not 12.5%). Kapos (UNEP-WCMC) gives about 22% (not 24%).

## 4. Relief statements found (by age)

- USGS (opened): Valley and Ridge, Paleozoic folded and faulted rocks, local relief "usually on the order of about 1000 feet" (about 300 m); elevations about 300 to 4000 ft (about 90 to 1220 m). Source: https://www.usgs.gov/centers/florence-bascom-geoscience-center/science/appalachian-basin-geologic-mapping-project
- USGS (opened): Appalachian Plateaus, average local relief "usually on the order of about 500 ft" (about 150 m), locally about 1000 ft in the New River Gorge (about 300 m). Highest point Spruce Knob 4862 ft (about 1482 m). Same source.
- World Bank box (opened, citing Price 1981): a mountain is "an elevated landform of high local relief e.g. 300 m"; many authors suggest 700 m; the text states that a relative relief of 900 m would include the Alps, Pyrenees, Caucasus, Himalaya, Andes, Rockies, Cascades and Sierra Nevada but NOT the Appalachians or the old degraded mountains of Scandinavia and the British Isles. Source: same World Bank PDF.
- World Bank box (opened, citing Hammond 1954, 1964): high mountains have local relief of 3000 ft (about 900 m); low mountains 1000 to 3000 ft (about 300 to 900 m), and the Appalachians are listed as low mountains. Same source.
- World Bank box (opened, citing a 1500 m relative relief criterion): the only areas in western Europe with relative relief above 1500 m are the Alps and the central Pyrenees; the Scandinavian and Carpathian mountains would be excluded. Same source.
- Braun and Robert 2005 (opened, Dabie Shan): mean topographic relief decreased by a factor of 2.5 to 4.5 over the last 60 to 80 Ma; mean exhumation rate 0.01 to 0.04 km/Ma; the most likely scenario has topography decreasing at a constant rate since about 100 Ma. Source: https://www.isterre.fr/IMG/pdf/Braun-and-Robert-ESPL2005.pdf
- Montgomery and Brandon 2002 (opened): local relief plays a minor role in landscape-scale erosion in active ranges; hillslopes adjust through landslide frequency; a non-linear mean slope-erosion relation with a kink above about 25 degrees (Olympic transect). Source: https://repository.geologyscience.ru/bitstreams/e6a139e0-9ca4-4e16-a238-3e7bea5bd406/download.
- Egholm et al. 2013 (abstract, opened): relief in old orogens is preserved for long times via a river-landslide feedback. Source: https://www.nature.com/articles/nature12218
- Himalaya (search summary only, source not identified): local relief above 6000 m and slopes above 30 degrees in Nepal; Tibetan Plateau maximum local relief about 2000 to 2500 m. UNVERIFIED as a primary value.
- Alps (search summary): relative relief above 1500 m in the Alps and central Pyrenees (World Bank box, above).
- Alps mean elevation: Wiki says the mean height of the peaks is about 2.5 km (summit mean, NOT the hypsometric mean of the range). Paleoelevation estimates for the Miocene conflict (see section 3).

Note on the GMBA definition (opened, Snethlage et al. 2022): the mountain test uses ruggedness (highest minus lowest elevation) inside 8 circular windows from about 250 m (1 pixel) to about 5 km (20 pixels), each with an empirically derived threshold (the minimum ruggedness at 920 random sample points). Relief at a single scale is therefore not a valid comparison; use several windows.

## 9. Key takeaways for the tool

1. Relief is scale-dependent. GMBA v2.0 measures ruggedness in windows from about 250 m to 5 km with empirical thresholds. The tool should compute local relief at several window sizes and compare each belt at the same scales.
2. Use survey-level local relief values where they exist: Valley and Ridge about 300 m, Plateau about 150 m (USGS). These are old, low-relief belts, far below the high-relief young belts. No survey value was found for the Himalaya or the Alps; treat those as UNVERIFIED until a DEM analysis is run.
3. Use the classic thresholds for "mountain": about 300 m (Price 1981) and 700 m (common suggestion) of local relief; high mountains 1000 to 1500 m relative relief (World Bank box). Under the 900 m relative-relief criterion the Appalachians are excluded from the mountain class, which matches the USGS "low mountain" reading.
4. Post-orogenic relief decay is slow and roughly exponential or linear: factor 2.5 to 4.5 over 60 to 100 Ma (Dabie Shan, Braun and Robert 2005). A simple model for the tool: relief decays by a factor of about 3 over about 80 Ma after the last deformation, with a rate that depends on flexural strength.
5. Erosion-relief is not linear: Montgomery and Brandon (2002) show a limiting relief (Hc) and a slope kink near 25 degrees. Cap hillslope gradients (about 25 to 35 degrees) for old belts rather than letting relief grow without a limit.
6. Old belts persist because of river-landslide feedback (Egholm et al. 2013). Do not force old belts to flatten to zero relief; keep a residual relief floor.
7. Mean elevation for the Alps is contested (Miocene paleoelevation 2350 to 2850 m vs present level reached about 5 to 6 Ma). For the tool, use a range rather than a single value for any young belt.
8. Peak heights are robust (Wiki, rounded): Himalaya 8849, Karakoram 8611, Andes 6961, Tien Shan 7439, Alps 4809, Zagros 4409, Atlas 4167, Southern Alps 3724, Taiwan 3952, Pyrenees 3404, Caucasus 5642, Apennines 2912, Carpathians 2656, Dinarides 2694, Scandes 2469, Appalachians 2037, Urals 1895, Massif Central 1886, Vosges 1424, Harz 1141, Drakensberg 3482, Western Ghats 2695, Cape Fold 2325. Scottish Highlands and Grenville peak values are still UNVERIFIED.
9. Belt age classes need correction in the brief (see section 1). A tool test set should use: young = Himalaya, Alps, Zagros, Taiwan, Southern Alps, Pyrenees, Apennines, Tien Shan; intermediate = Urals, Appalachians, Cape Fold Belt; old = Caledonides, Scandes, Scottish Highlands, Variscan massifs, Grenville.
10. Remaining gaps before the table is complete: crest and summit elevations, mean elevations, and local relief for almost every belt. The cheapest way to fill them is to compute them from a DEM (for example the GMBA v2.0 shapefiles at https://earthenv.org/mountains, the DOI 10.48601/earthenv-t9k2-1407 cited in the search summary) rather than from text sources, which did not contain these numbers.
