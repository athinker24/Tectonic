## 1. Egholm, Knudsen & Sandiford 2013, Nature 498:475-478, "Lifespan of mountain ranges scaled by feedbacks between landsliding and erosion by rivers"
Source: https://msandifo.github.io/pdfs/peer/2013_nature12218.pdf (read full text, Letter + Fig. 3 caption)
- Stream-power (SPIM) models give a gradual decline of erosion after uplift stops; the saltation-abrasion model with landslide feedback keeps peaks high for longer. Fig. 3 caption and text: "peaks can stand above an elevation of 2 km for more than 200 Myr following the cessation of tectonic activity" (saltation-abrasion); the scaled stream-power model keeps elevations above 2 km only ~20 Myr. Model runs cover 400 Myr of post-orogenic evolution.
- Transport-limited steady state: stream-power reaches it after ~25 Myr; saltation-abrasion takes "more than ten times longer".
- Paper says the Appalachians (Palaeozoic) preserve km-scale relief; the authors note the mechanism is a proposed physical explanation; results depend on parameterization ("These precise figures depend on the details of the parameterization").
- Assumptions: 2D-ish landscape sim with flexural isostasy, stochastic bedrock landsliding with threshold slope, saltation-abrasion incision, no climate/lithology variation in the main experiment, no tectonic uplift after cessation.
- Note: the sigma "groups of mountains" title in the brief does not exist; the paper is titled as above.

## 2. Gallen, Wegmann & Bohnenstiehl 2013, GSA Today 23(2):4-10, "Miocene rejuvenation of topographic relief in the southern Appalachians"
Source: https://rock.geosociety.org/net/gsatoday/archive/23/2/pdf/i1052-5173-23-2-4.pdf (read)
- Cullasaja River basin (~300 km2, western NC), 6 m LiDAR. Relief increased ">150% since the Miocene" (abstract).
- Knickpoint-based paleo-relief reconstruction: paleo-landscape ~300 +/- 25 m below modern relief per the text (lines on paleo-relief; treat as the basin-scale estimate).
- Argues against climate as the primary driver (timing begins before the late Cenozoic transition to glacial-interglacial, <4 Myr); favors epeirogenic uplift in the Miocene. Competing with Hack (1960) dynamic equilibrium (Matmon et al. 2003 cited as supporting equilibrium).

## 3. Matmon, Bierman, Larsen, Southworth, Pavich, Finkel & Caffee 2003, AJS 303:817-855, "Erosion of an ancient mountain range, the Great Smoky Mountains, North Carolina and Tennessee"
Source: pdf read from https://ajsonline.org/article/61707.pdf (abstract and erosion rate section)
- 10Be and 26Al in bedrock (n=10), colluvium, alluvium (n=59). Spatially homogeneous erosion rates "25-30 mm/kyr" throughout GSMNP. Headwater alluvium average 28 +/- 8 mm/kyr; outlet rivers 24 +/- 6 mm/kyr (n=16).
- Bedrock average 29 +/- 13 mm/kyr (34 +/- 9 for sandstone).
- Conclusion: rates integrated over different time frames are similar; rates appear to be slow and spatially uniform; controlled by subsurface bedrock erosion and diffusive slope processes.
- Converted: 25-30 mm/kyr = 25-30 m/Myr.
- The Geology letter (Matmon et al. 2003, Geology 31:155-158, "Temporally and spatially uniform rates of erosion...") reports 27 +/- 4 m/Myr (from search snippet; UNVERIFIED in full text; source: https://pubs.usgs.gov/publication/70025897 search snippet).

- Gallen et al. 2013 (same paper), denudation from mean local relief, relation to Ahnert (1970) global mean relief vs denudation: active portion 27 +/- 11 mm/kyr, relict portion 6 +/- 6 mm/kyr (source: https://rock.geosociety.org/net/gsatoday/archive/23/2/pdf/i1052-5173-23-2-4.pdf, text near Fig. 2 and the DR1 supplementary reference).

## 4. Braun & Robert 2005, ESPL 30:1203-1225, "Constraints on the rate of post-orogenic erosional decay from low-temperature thermochronological data: application to the Dabie Shan, China"
Source: https://www.isterre.fr/IMG/pdf/Braun-and-Robert-ESPL2005.pdf (read abstract, intro, Eq. 23, tau tests, conclusions)
- Decay law assumed (Eq. 23): h = h0 + h0(beta-1)[1 - exp((t - te)/tau)]/[1 - exp(-te/tau)], tau = "erosional response time" (time for relief to drop by factor e). te = 100 Ma, elastic plate 40 km in tau tests; tau tested 10, 20, 40, 100 Ma and infinity.
- Dabie Shan best fit (abstract): relief decreased by a factor ~2.5 over the last 60-70 Ma, mean exhumation rate ~0.02 km/Ma (0.01-0.04 km/Ma range, abstract says "0.01 to 0.04 km Ma-1"). Trade-off: 0.01 km/Ma with relief loss 4.5 fits only a few percent worse.
- Their first-pass estimate gives beta - 1 = 4 (relief loss factor) with "at least 70 Ma ago" end of tectonics.
- Key result: the best-fit model is NOT exponential; data "support ... rate of exhumation ... more or less constant during the post-orogenic phase"; "not compatible with an exponential decrease in relief" with erosion proportional to relief. The text says the most likely scenario has topography decreasing at roughly constant rate since ~100 Ma ago. Authors say this conflicts with the assumption that erosion scales with relief.
- Assumptions: 2D heat transport with flexure and changing topography; lithosphere elastic thickness trade-off (10 to 30 km range in abstract); geothermal gradient insensitive. Erosion via age-elevation spectral method.
- Converted: 0.02 km/Ma = 20 m/Myr; 0.01 to 0.04 km/Ma = 10 to 40 m/Myr.

## 5. Fox, McKeon & Shuster 2014, G3 15:4217-4229 (NOT the EPSL paper named in the brief)
Source: https://authors.library.caltech.edu/50829/7/Fox_et_al-2014-Geochemistry,_Geophysics,_Geosystems.pdf (read intro, Fig. 8 caption)
- Methods paper on 3-D parent nuclide zonation in apatite 4He/3He. It does not itself give a decay time constant.
- Intro: long-term erosion from low-T thermochronometry "approximately 20 m/Ma" across Appalachian localities (Blackmer 1994; Boettcher & Milliken 1994; Naeser 2004; McKeon 2014; etc.), consistent with cosmogenic rates (Matmon 2003; Hancock & Kirwan 2007; Portenga 2013).
- Uses sample SY-2 (Waterrock Knob, Blue Ridge, ~2000 m ridge top): McKeon et al. (2014) thermal history = steady cooling over last 180 Myr at 0.4 C/Myr, "erosion rate of 20 m/Ma sustained for the last 180 Ma" (Fox et al. text).

## 8. Liu 2014, Nature Geoscience (volume not verified), "Rejuvenation of Appalachian topography caused by subsidence-induced differential erosion" (DOI 10.1038/NGEO2187)
Source: https://www.nature.com via PDF at https://par.nsf.gov/servlets/purl/10325500 (read abstract and intro)
- Argues Appalachian relief rejuvenation since the Palaeogene was caused by mantle-induced dynamic SUBSIDENCE (sinking Farallon slab), not uplift. Model predicts 400 m of relief and >200 m of elevation developed via flexural-isostatic response to subsidence-induced differential erosion. Gulf of Mexico sediment and Miocene erosion pulses used as evidence.
- Dynamic topography cases 1-3 disagree on sign for offshore NJ; cases 1-2 show subsidence along the Appalachians.
- Relevance: a competing mechanism to Gallen et al. (uplift) and Miller et al. (base-level fall).

## 9. Miller, Sak, Kirby & Bierman 2013, EPSL 369-370:1-12, "Neogene rejuvenation of central Appalachian topography: evidence for differential rock uplift from stream profiles and erosion rates" (DOI 10.1016/j.epsl.2013.04.007)
Source: abstract read at https://pure.psu.edu/en/publications/neogene-rejuvenation-of-central-appalachian-topography-evidence-f/ (NOT AJS as the brief said)
- Susquehanna basin: knickpoints cluster at 300-600 m elevation. 10Be erosion rates ~5-30 m/Myr above knickpoints, ~50-100 m/Myr below.
- Channel steepness scales roughly linearly with erosion rate. Interpreted as a transient wave from ~100-150 m of relative base-level fall due to epeirogenic uplift; onset predates ~3.5 Ma and possibly ~15 Ma; attributed to mantle dynamics.
- Rejects climate change and drainage reorganization as the main cause (per authors).

## 11. Olivetti et al. 2020, Lithosphere 12(1):133-149, French Massif Central (Hercynian, foreland)
Source: https://www.research.unipd.it/bitstream/11577/3329366/2/133.pdf (read abstract and relevant lines)
- Massif Central reaches ~1700 m asl and ~1000 m of relief; not explained by Alpine or extensional tectonics. Eastern flank profile 1200 m elevation.
- Two-step cooling history: first exhumation with onset in the Cretaceous; second Cenozoic phase with likely post-Eocene onset, associated with relief formation and valley incision, "possibly induced by a long-wavelength domal uplift supported by mantle upwelling".
- Massif Central is wider and higher than Bohemian, Rhenish, Vosges/Black Forest massifs; more intense late Cenozoic volcanism.
- Numerical rate: propagation of the erosion wave estimated at ~0.1-1 km/Myr (this is a propagation rate of the front, not denudation; authors' text, lines ~1396-1401).

## 15. Isostatic compensation ratio (brief's "0.15 to 0.17")
Source: Champagnac, Schlunegger, Norton, von Blanckenburg, Abbuehl & Schwab 2009, Tectonophysics 474:236-249, "Erosion-driven uplift of the modern Central Alps" (DOI 10.1016/j.tecto.2009.02.024). Read from https://gfzpublic.gfz-potsdam.de/pubman/item/item_238796_1/component/file_238795/13194.pdf
- Text: with crust rho ~2.8 and mantle rho ~3.25 g/cm3 "yielding an isostatic response of ~5/6 (~80%) to erosional unloading"; "a ratio between erosion and rock uplift rates close to 0.8 is expected for purely erosion-driven rock uplift in case of zero (or negligible) flexural rigidity." Central Alps observed slope 0.88 +/- 0.14.
- My arithmetic (not in the source): with Airy compensation the surface drops by E(1 - rho_c/rho_m) per unit eroded thickness E, giving 0.14 for (2.8, 3.25) and 0.18 for (2.7, 3.3). So net elevation loss is ~0.14-0.18 km per km eroded, consistent with the brief's 0.15-0.17. The brief's number is therefore the NET fraction, not the rebound fraction. Flexure with Te > 0 reduces the rebound fraction (more net loss in the sense of less rebound) per Champagnac et al. text.
- Molnar & England 1990 (Nature 346:29-34, https://www.nature.com/articles/346029a0.pdf) abstract only read; the 0.15-0.17 figure was NOT found in it (UNVERIFIED in that paper).
- Champagnac et al. 2007 Geology (DOI 10.1130/G23053A.1, "Quaternary erosion-induced isostatic rebound in the western Alps"): snippet says about half of 1.1 mm/yr uplift in southern Valais is erosion-induced (UNVERIFIED, full text not read).

## Derived numbers (my arithmetic, NOT stated in the sources)
- If erosion is linear in relief, E = k*h, then the e-folding time is tau = 1/k = h/E. Example: h = 1 km, E = 20 m/Myr gives tau = 50 Myr; h = 2 km, E = 20 m/Myr gives tau = 100 Myr. Relief lost in 100 Myr at a constant E = 20 m/Myr is 2 km, so a linear decline (Braun & Robert best fit) and an exponential one with tau = 100 Myr differ little over 100 Myr.
- Braun & Robert Eq. 23 with te = 100 Ma: tau = 10 Ma gives very rapid early decay; tau = infinity is linear decline.
- Converting the literature: 1 m/Myr = 1 mm/kyr = 0.001 km/Myr.

## Disagreements and open issues
1. Appalachian relief origin: dynamic equilibrium / old relief with low denudation (Matmon 2003; McKeon 2014 pre-Cenozoic cooling; Portenga 2013) vs Miocene/Cenozoic rejuvenation by uplift (Gallen 2013; Miller 2013 with 100-150 m base-level fall) vs subsidence (Liu 2014) vs climate (Molnar 2004; Hancock & Kirwan 2007 as cited by Gallen 2013). Gallen 2013 explicitly says cosmogenic studies have not reached consensus.
2. Decay law: Braun & Robert (2005) data prefer near-linear relief loss over exponential; models with erosion proportional to relief give exponential. Egholm 2013 says stream-power models decay fast (~25 Myr to steady state) vs saltation-abrasion.
3. Decay time: 1-10 Myr (SPIM, Baldwin 2003) vs 100s Myr (with threshold, sediment, or landslide feedback). The 2022 Nature abstract says outcome depends on erosional efficiency.
4. Denudation of old orogens: 6 +/- 6 mm/kyr (Gallen relict) vs 20 m/Myr (McKeon, Fox) vs 9 m/Myr ridges (Portenga); Matmon 25-30 m/Myr. Measured values range 5-30 m/Myr for old, low-relief ridges.
5. Scandinavia: Japsen/Green/Hendriks not found; Hendriks & Redfield (2005, EPSL) argue AFT annealing underestimated, so denudation overestimated (search snippet only, UNVERIFIED). Medvedev (2015) < 10 m/Myr Carboniferous-Cretaceous (snippet only).

## Key takeaways for the tool
1. Decay time depends mostly on the erosion law. Pure stream-power (E = K A^m S^n, no threshold) gives 1-25 Myr for the bulk of relief decay (Baldwin 2003; Egholm 2013). A threshold (critical shear stress, ~x20 in Baldwin's example), sediment cover, or landslide feedback is needed to keep km-scale relief for 100-300 Myr (Egholm 2013 >200 Myr; Baldwin 2003 >100 Ma combined). So the owner's requirement (old belts low and rounded, young belts high) needs a threshold or hillslope process, not just stream power.
2. Isostasy: each km eroded lowers the surface by only ~0.14-0.18 km under Airy compensation (my arithmetic from density 2.7-2.8 and 3.25-3.3; Champagnac et al. 2009 ratio ~0.8). Flexure with Te 10-40 km changes this (Braun & Robert 2005). Implement isostatic rebound in the code, and that alone makes elevation decay slower than erosion.
3. Calibration targets from read sources: ridge and outcrop denudation for old Appalachian-type belts 5-30 m/Myr (Portenga 2013; Matmon 2003); mean denudation ~20 m/Myr over 100+ Myr (McKeon via Gallen & Pazzaglia; Fox 2014); old-belt exhumation 10-40 m/Myr for Dabie Shan (Braun & Robert 2005); active or rejuvenating channels 50-100 m/Myr below knickpoints (Miller 2013). Use these as checks on the simulated late-stage erosion rate.
4. Relief-denudation relation (Ahnert 1970, via Gallen 2013): erosion roughly linear in local relief; if you use E = k*h, tau = 1/k. Example: k = 0.02 per Myr gives tau = 50 Myr.
5. Avoid assuming exponential decay in the output test: Braun & Robert (2005) best fit for Dabie Shan is nearly linear decline, so the tool should be checked against age-elevation data rather than only an e-folding time.
6. Do not rely on the brief's citation details without checks: Fox et al. 2014 (G3, not EPSL), Miller et al. 2013 (EPSL, not AJS), Egholm 2013 title (not "Groups of mountains"), Matmon et al. 2003 Geology vs AJS (both exist, Geology version 27 m/Myr from snippet), Baldwin 2003 authors (Baldwin, Whipple & Tucker, not Willett & Schlunegger), Pazzaglia & Brandon 1996 (not found).
