## 3. Hillslope gradient, local relief and the threshold debate

### 3.1 Threshold hillslopes and erosion rate
- Burbank et al. 1996 (NW Himalaya): "the average angles of hillslopes are steep and essentially independent of erosion rate, suggesting control by a common threshold process." [S] https://experts.nau.edu/en/publications/bedrock-incision-rock-uplift-and-threshold-hillslopes-in-the-nort/ (search summary); Indus incision rates 2-12 mm/yr from dated surfaces (same search summary) [S].
- Montgomery and Brandon 2002 (EPSL 201:481-489, Olympics + global): erosion rates adjust mainly through landslide frequency, not hillslope steepness; long-term erosion vs mean slope is non-linear; Olympic analysis used a 10 m DEM. [S] https://repository.geologyscience.ru/handle/123456789/28097 (copy, not opened; the full text was blocked, see section 9). A search snippet (source not reliably identified) cites the threshold as roughly 35 to 40 deg for steep ranges [S].
- DiBiase et al. 2010 (San Gabriel Mountains, 50 basins, detrital 10Be; erosion 35 to 1100 m/Ma per search): catchment mean hillslope angle increases with erosion rate up to about 300 m/Ma then becomes invariant, while channel steepness keeps rising. [S] same source. The 30 deg value is [U].
- Liu et al. 2021 (SE Tibet, SRTM 30 m): 82% of slopes 30 +/- 5 deg, threshold taken as 30 deg; 4,430 landslides, 71% in areas of excess topography above 30 deg; excess topography volume correlates linearly with basin mean erosion rate (r = 0.83, n = 62). Authors state 30 deg is a "reasonable range", not a derived constant. [R] https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/earth-science/articles/10.3389/feart.2021.684365/pdf
- Larsen (UW dissertation, search): landslide erosion decouples from gradient above about 30 deg, with landslide scaling depending on hillslope material. [S] https://intranet.ess.uw.edu/content/people/student_publications_files/james-larsen--isaac/Larsen_washington_0250E_12187.pdf (repository copy; not opened).
- Global modal slopes of 30 to 35 deg in steep landscapes (EGU 2017 abstract, search). [S] https://meetingorganizer.copernicus.org/EGU2017/EGU2017-16074.pdf

### 3.2 Critical gradient Sc and the 30 deg number (conversion is mine)
Sc is the tangent of the threshold angle (dimensionless). Conversions below are arithmetic, not from the papers.
- Roering et al. 1999 (Oregon Coast Range): Sc = 1.2 [R via Grieve 2016 text] (Sc 1.2 = 50.2 deg).
- Hurst et al. 2012 (Cascade Ridge): Sc = 0.8 (38.7 deg), as quoted in Grieve 2016.
- Grieve et al. 2016: Sc = 0.79 for one site and 0.72 for Cascade Ridge (35.8 deg), and say Sc = 1.2 "is too large" for the Oregon site. [R] https://esurf.copernicus.org/articles/4/309/2016/esurf-4-309-2016.pdf
- 30 deg corresponds to Sc = 0.577.
So the same landscape class is assigned thresholds between about 36 and 50 deg by different authors. This is a live disagreement.

### 3.3 Mean gradient and erosion in Alpine catchments
Stutenbecker et al. 2016 Table 1, 50 catchments (10 to 674 km2) in the upper Rhone basin, 2 m swissALTI3D DEM [R]. My summary of the table:
- Catchment mean slope: 19.6 to 40.7 deg, median 30.3 deg.
- Local relief (1 km radius, per text): 471 to 991 m, median 736 m.
- Hypsometric integral: 0.28 to 0.70, median 0.45.
- Authors' conclusion: variations of elevation, slope and relief follow long-term uplift differences, while lithology shapes landform. https://esurf.copernicus.org/articles/4/253/2016/esurf-4-253-2016.pdf

### 3.4 Caucasus, Zagros, Pyrenees, Taiwan (search only)
- Caucasus: Forte et al. 2016 report channel steepness vs mean hillslope gradient relation invariant along strike, implying no along-strike gradient in erosion or uplift. [S] https://asu.elsevierpure.com/en/publications/decoupling-of-modern-shortening-rates-climate-and-topography-in-t/ . A later 10Be study (journal not identified) reports ksn vs erosion rate strongly non-linear, with topography insensitive above about 500 m/Myr. [S] same source family.
- Pyrenees: Fox et al. 2023 (GRL, doi 10.1029/2022GL101995): channel steepness similar between bedrock and softer lithologies despite low-relief surfaces. [S] https://doaj.org/article/c00325bc1adf4c5490ef99a033f8f3e2 . Fox et al. 2020: exhumation of harder rock reduces upstream steepness and erosion. [S]
- Taiwan: steady-state HI near 0.5 (Chen et al. 2012 Geomorphology, search summary) [S] https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012Geomo.171....1C/abstract
- Taiwan erosion rates 3-6 mm/yr active mountains (Dadson et al. 2003 Nature, search summary) [S] https://nora.nerc.ac.uk/id/eprint/11369/ ; Dadson and Hovius 2004 cite 3-7 mm/yr [S].

## 4. Hilltop curvature (ridge sharpness)

- Hurst et al. 2012 (JGR-ES 117:F02017, doi 10.1029/2011JF002057): hilltop curvature scales linearly with erosion rate under a nonlinear sediment flux law; 1 m DEM; Cascade Ridge and Feather River data. [S] https://www.pure.ed.ac.uk/ws/files/8252430/PDF_Using_Hilltop_Curvature...2012.pdf (copy; not opened)
- Gabet et al. 2021 (JGR-ES 126, doi 10.1029/2020JF005858; ESSOAr preprint): hilltop curvature increases with the square root of erosion rate, not linearly. [S] https://essopenarchive.org/users/560833/articles/608657-hilltop-curvature-increases-with-the-square-root-of-erosion-rate
- Struble and Roering (preprint, ESurf Discussions esurf-2021-40) [R]: lidar DEMs about 1 m, lambda 5-141 m. Both PFT (polynomial) and wavelet CHT "systematically underestimate CHT at moderate to high erosion rates" and approximate a square-root relation. In synthetic tests CHT is underestimated by more than 20% for E* = 30 and by 60% for E* = 100 when lambda = 15 m. https://esurf.copernicus.org/preprints/esurf-2021-40/esurf-2021-40.pdf
- Reported OCR (Oregon Coast Range) erosion rates in that preprint: 0.007 to 0.178 mm/yr among catchments [R].
- Disagreement: linear (Hurst 2012) vs square-root (Gabet 2021; Struble and Roering 2021). Also Grieve et al. 2016 use hilltop curvature from manually picked hilltops with gradient below 0.05 Sc [R].

## 5. Drainage area scaling (Hack's law) and drainage density

- Hack 1957 (Virginia and Maryland, 0.12 to 375 mi2, Appalachian, Piedmont, Coastal Plain; more than 100 field localities, no DEM) [R]: "length ... increases directly as the 0.6 power of the drainage area. This rate of increase is not affected, except locally and for short distances, by the geology of the basin." So L ~ A^0.6. Slope relation: channel slope proportional to the 0.6 power of (median grain size / drainage area). https://pubs.usgs.gov/pp/0294b/report.pdf
- Drainage density: no numeric compilation found by mountain age. Champagnac, Molnar and Sue (EGU 2009, 2010) compiled more than 50 belts; relief vs erosion-related correlations about 0.7 overall, about 0.5 for convergent belts [S] (URLs in search log). DiBiase et al. 2012 report similar drainage density across the San Gabriel erosion range [S].
- Status: drainage density by age class is UNVERIFIED. Need Montgomery and Dietrich 1989 or Champagnac compilation.

## 6. Valley and ridge spacing
- Perron et al. 2009 (Nature): valley spacing scales with the length where diffusive creep and fluvial incision timescales match; five field sites; spacing 30 to 300 m; Napa Valley (Franciscan Complex) 128 +/- 23 m. [S] https://ideas.repec.org/a/nat/nature/v460y2009i7254d10.1038_nature08174.html ; the proportionality constant is UNVERIFIED.
- Perron et al. 2008 (JGR 113): uniform valley spacing emerges from competition for drainage area at topographic equilibrium; the scale is set by first-order basin area. [S] https://earthref.org/ERR/120961/
- Relevance for the tool: spacing is a fingerprint of diffusivity/erodibility, not of age directly. A tool could check that valley spacing is in the 30 to 300 m range at 30 to 90 m DEM resolution (inference, not from source).

## 7. Hypsometry

- Strahler (1952) convention (search): convex high-integral curves = young, undissected; concave low-integral = old, dissected. [S] https://georeview.usv.ro/wp-content/uploads/2023/05/Article.7-Vol.25-1.pdf
- Greater Khingan Mountains (old-age cited, NE China), 90 m SRTM: HI 0.13 to 0.44, mean 0.30, mostly concave curves, "approaching the old-age development stage". [R] https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/feart.2021.796610
- Swiss Alps (Stutenbecker 2016 Table 1): HI 0.28 to 0.70, median 0.45. [R]
- Caveats from search: mature and old stages can produce identical HI curves unless monadnocks are present [S] https://researchclopedia.vercel.app/research/W2064972007 ; a review argues youth/maturity are poorly suited to modern quantitative use [S] https://esurf.copernicus.org/preprints/3/1061/2015/esurfd-3-1061-2015-AR1.pdf
- Pyrenees, Zagros, Caucasus, Appalachian, Urals, Scottish HI values: NOT FOUND in the time available. [gap]

## 8. Channel steepness (ksn), concavity and knickpoints

### 8.1 Method
- Mudd et al. 2018 (ESurf 6:505) [R]: S = ks A^-theta; the reference concavity is typically chosen by regression of log S on log A. Where uplift is higher in a range core, the correct concavity is lower than the apparent slope-area concavity (their simulations). Kirby and Whipple 2001 found elevated apparent concavities in the Siwalik Hills (India and Nepal) where uplift falls downstream. Example basin (Loess Plateau, SRTM 30 m): theta about 0.45. https://esurf.copernicus.org/articles/6/505/2018/esurf-6-505-2018.pdf
- Mudd 2018 also notes ks correlates with erosion rate in several studies (Ouimet 2009; DiBiase 2010; Scherler 2014; Mandal 2015; Harel 2016) [R, intro].
- Grieve et al. 2016 (above) use a different normalised metric, relief and erosion, not ksn [R].
- Fox et al. 2026 (Eos highlight, search): the fixed catchment-area threshold separating channels from hillslopes can bias ksn when uplift lengthens hillslopes. [S]

### 8.2 Regional ksn numbers
- Taiwan, Chen, Shyu, Chang 2015 (Tectonics 34:2249-2270, doi 10.1002/2014TC003795) [S]: 20 eastern-flank rivers; low ksn at both orogen ends, steep rivers with tectonic knickpoints in the collisional centre. Same ksn-erosion trend as other orogens but at significantly higher erosion rates, attributed to debris flows and landslides. EGU 2013 abstract (21 rivers, different count): ksn 60 to 570, mean about 190 (units not stated in snippet). https://tdr.lib.ntu.edu.tw/handle/123456789/58730
- Himalaya: Wobus et al. 2006 (Tectonics, doi 10.1029/2005TC001935) is "Neotectonics of the central Nepalese Himalaya" [S]; ksn values not retrieved [U].
- Zagros (Obaid and Allen 2019, Tectonophysics, doi 10.1016/j.tecto.2019.05.024) [S]: ksn above 50 m^0.9 near the upper elevation limit of seismogenic thrusting (about 1250 m); higher ksn in Bakhtyari Culmination, rare in Fars.
- Caucasus: see 3.4.
- Southern Alps (Whataroa, Alpine Fault): long-term exhumation 8.7 +/- 1.3 mm/yr east of the Alpine Fault; catchment 10Be denudation 6.6 +/- 1.7 mm/yr (Larsen et al. 2014, search). [S] https://par.nsf.gov/servlets/purl/10635927 . Landslide denudation 9 +/- 4 mm/yr (Hovius et al. 1997, search). [S]
- Appalachians (Miller et al. 2013 northern Appalachians, search): normalised gradient scales linearly with catchment-mean erosion rate; interpreted as transient adjustment. Blue Ridge knickpoints: 45 on 41 tributaries clustered at 700 to 900 m. [S]
- Scotland (Durham/SEPA, search): no correlation between normalised ksn and denudation rates (0.175 to 1.356 mm/yr). Attributed to inhomogeneous erosion and postglacial isostatic uplift. [S] https://durham-repository.worktribe.com/OutputFile/3746929 (repository copy; not opened)
- Old orogens (Urals, Scandes, Hercynian): NOT FOUND.

### 8.3 Knickpoints
- Appalachian knickpoint clustering and about 200 m relative base-level fall (search): see 8.2 [S].
- Scotland: knickpoint retreat may not record isostatic uplift alone; basin size and discharge also control retreat [S] (Robertson thesis).
- Relict-landscape caveat: steepened reaches upstream of knickpoints shrink apparent relict area (Berlin and Anderson 2009, open access, Roan Plateau) [S].

## 9. Glacial buzzsaw and glacially limited height

- Egholm et al. 2009 (Nature 460:884-887): maximum mountain height correlates with snowline gradients across ranges; glacial sliding above the snowline plus isostatic response drives elevation toward a window just below the snowline. [S] https://ideas.repec.org/a/nat/nature/v460y2009i7257d10.1038_nature08263.html
- Mitchell and Montgomery 2006 (Quaternary Research 65:96-107, doi 10.1016/j.yqres.2005.08.018): summit altitudes in the Washington Cascades capped by glacial and paraglacial erosion above the Quaternary ELA. [S] https://scholarhub.vn/publication/:slug/0c885066-8724-440c-b906-0bc23b778a51
- Brozovic et al. 1997 (Science 276:571-574): glacial and periglacial limit on altitude and relief in belts crossing the snowline [S]. Cunningham et al. 2019 cite Brozovic 1997 for peaks rising more than 1 km above the ELA [R].
- Critique: a Quaternary Research forum piece (search) argues plateau remnants used as buzzsaw evidence are often dissected pre-glacial relief. [S] https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/quaternary-research/article/glacial-and-periglacial-buzzsaws-fitting-mechanisms-to-metaphors/4828F3C917E238FD0405E6A0B9AF51CF
- Cunningham et al. 2019 [R]: in the tropics, many high ranges show glacial limitation tied to the perched base level at the ELA, with cyclic truncation toward the cold-phase ELA; they conclude that glacial limitation is disequilibrium on timescales shorter than the glaciation cycle. https://esurf.copernicus.org/articles/7/147/2019/esurf-7-147-2019.pdf
- Forte 2016, Caucasus: maintenance of high topography in the western Greater Caucasus may relate to dynamic topography. [S]

Tool implication: a glacial-limit test requires a snowline/ELA input; the tool cannot test "height limited at the snowline" without a climate field.

## 10. Automated realism and terrain checks

### 10.1 PTRM (Rajasekaran et al.)
- Data: SRTM 3 arc-sec (about 90 m) tiles, 512 x 512 px per tile, about 100 km per tile, so about 200 m per pixel. [R] https://arxiv.org/pdf/1909.04610
- Geomorphons: 10 classes (flat, peak, ridge, shoulder, spur, slope, footslope, valley, hollow, pit). Pixel scale about 200 m, so geomorphon histograms depend on DEM resolution and window. [R]
- Scores 0 to 1 (1 = perfect realism): Arizona real terrain 0.76; thermal-erosion synthetic 0.51; example real vs synthetic pairs 0.75, 0.77, 0.69. [R]
- Limit: PTRM is a perceptual score, fitted to forced-choice rankings on rendered images; it does not measure age, slope or relief. Use as a sanity check only.

### 10.2 Orometry (Argudo et al. 2019)
- Definitions (Funke et al. 2023 read, consistent with Argudo et al. 2019 terms in search): isolation = distance from a peak to the nearest point of higher elevation (isolation limit point); prominence = height above the key col. [R] https://publikationen.bibliothek.kit.edu/1000163542/151556993
- Argudo 2019 numeric statistics by range: NOT RETRIEVED. The PDF copy at UCT was in a refused batch (section 9 log). [gap]

### 10.3 Terrain descriptors (Argudo et al. 2025)

## 11. Disagreements (summary)
1. Hillslope gradient vs erosion: threshold (Burbank 1996; Montgomery and Brandon 2002; Liu 2021) vs continuous/linear soil-transport fit with threshold (a San Gabriel study, search) vs saturation at about 300 m/Ma (DiBiase 2010). [S]
2. Critical gradient Sc: 1.2 (Roering 1999) vs 0.8 (Hurst 2012) vs 0.72 to 0.79 (Grieve 2016). Converts to 36 to 50 deg. [R]
3. Hilltop curvature vs erosion: linear (Hurst 2012) vs square-root (Gabet 2021; Struble and Roering preprint). [R/S]
4. Glacial buzzsaw: strong limit (Egholm 2009; Brozovic 1997; Mitchell and Montgomery 2006) vs critique (Quaternary Research commentary; Cunningham 2019 frames glacial limitation as cyclic and disequilibrium, not as a fixed ceiling). [R/S]
5. Concavity: single theta (about 0.45 in Mudd 2018 example) vs uplift-dependent theta; slope-area vs chi methods disagree. [R]
6. Taiwan: ksn-erosion trend same as other orogens but at higher erosion (Chen 2015) vs Dadson 2003 finds highest erosion where relief is low, controlled by storms and weak rock. [S]
7. Scotland: normalised ksn does not correlate with denudation (Durham/SEPA) vs positive correlations in other orogens. [S]
8. Hypsometry as age proxy: used widely (Khingan, Strahler) vs review says maturity/youth poorly suited. [S]
9. Khingan HI 0.30 labelled "old", while the same study says tectonics moderately stable, so HI is not a clean age proxy. [R]

## 16. Key takeaways for the tool

1. Use a threshold-aware gradient test, not a single slope number. Measured catchment-mean slopes in glaciated or active Alpine catchments cluster near 30 deg (Stutenbecker 2016 median 30.3 deg, range 19.6 to 40.7 deg, 2 m DEM). Landslide-threshold regions cluster at 30 +/- 5 deg (Liu 2021). A tool gate of roughly 30 to 40 deg for "steep but threshold" terrain is supported; the upper limit is disputed (Sc 0.72 to 1.2 = 36 to 50 deg across Grieve 2016 and Roering 1999).
2. Local relief in a 1 km window: Alpine catchments median about 740 m (471 to 991 m) at 2 m DEM. Old-belt numbers (Khingan) were not given as relief; only HI. Relief targets for old belts are NOT established by this run.
3. Hypsometric integral is a usable but ambiguous gate. Old-age example HI 0.13 to 0.44 (mean 0.30; Khingan, 90 m DEM). Young glaciated Alpine HI median 0.45 (range 0.28 to 0.70). Taiwan steady-state about 0.5 (search). Use HI as a loose band, not an age classifier.
4. Hack's law: L ~ A^0.6 (Hack 1957, Appalachian-Piedmont). Check the tool's river network against this exponent. Expect deviations in young, steep terrain; Hack's data are not from active belts.
5. Hilltop curvature: linear vs square-root disagreement (Hurst 2012 vs Gabet 2021; Struble and Roering preprint). Do not validate ridge sharpness with one linear calibration; use a range. Resolution matters: the 2021 analysis uses 1 m lidar; their synthetic test shows 20 to 60% underestimation at high E*.
6. Concavity: theta near 0.45 is a typical example, but the reference value depends on uplift gradient (Mudd 2018). If the tool uses uniform uplift, expect the apparent theta to be biased; in a tool-generated range with high core uplift, the true theta is lower than a regression through slope-area data.
7. ksn fingerprints found: Zagros above 50 m^0.9 near 1250 m (snippet); Taiwan 60 to 570 with mean about 190 (2013 abstract, units unstated). These are search-only and not calibrated across studies. Do not use as acceptance bounds without the originals.
8. Valley spacing 30 to 300 m (Perron 2009 field sites). Check that generated valley spacing falls in this band for the tool's grid size; the proportionality constant is unverified.
9. Glacial buzzsaw test requires a snowline/ELA input. Cunningham et al. 2019 gives a concrete alternative test: hypsometric maximum relative to warm-phase and cold-phase ELA. Egholm 2009 claims height tracks snowline; this is contested.
10. PTRM: 0 to 1 perceptual score on 512 x 512 px tiles at about 200 m per pixel (SRTM 90 m source). Real Arizona terrain 0.76, thermal-erosion synthetic 0.51. Usable as a black-box realism score, not as an age test.
11. Isolation and prominence (Argudo 2019 orometry; Funke et al. 2023 algorithm): isolation is distance to the nearest higher point; prominence is height above the key col. Both can be computed from any DEM. Numeric targets by range NOT found.
12. Scottish and Appalachian ksn-erosion relations are weak or disputed (Scotland no correlation; Appalachian linear normalised gradient). Old-belt fingerprints in ksn remain a gap.
