## Sources read (running log)

### Read directly (primary text)
- PTRM preprint, Rajasekaran, Kang, Galin, Guerin, Peytavie, Benes, arXiv:1909.04610, https://arxiv.org/pdf/1909.04610
  - Real terrains from SRTM 3 arc-sec (about 90 m) tiles, 512 x 512 px per tile, about 100 km x 100 km per tile, so about 200 m per px (the paper says "around 200 meters per pixel"). Source: same PDF, section 5 "Terrain data".
  - Geomorphons: 10 classes (flat, peak/summit, ridge, shoulder, spur, slope, footslope, valley, hollow, pit/depression). Computed with an open implementation; their pixel size is about 200 m, so geomorphon scale is tied to DEM resolution (PTRM p. section 4).
  - Reported scores: real Arizona terrain PTRM = 0.76; thermal-erosion synthetic PTRM = 0.51; other examples 0.75, 0.77, 0.69 (preprint Fig. 1 and later figure panels). Score scale 0 to 1, 1 = perfect realism. Preprint = arXiv version; the published version is ACM TAP 19(2) art. 6, 2022, DOI 10.1145/3514244 (from search metadata, not read).
  - Caveat: PTRM is a perceptual score fitted to two-alternative forced choice experiments on rendered images of real vs synthetic terrains; it says nothing about hillslope gradient or age. Geomorphon histograms are a resolution-dependent descriptor.

- Hack 1957, USGS PP 294-B, pubs.usgs.gov/pp/0294b/report.pdf (read, text extracted). Streams 0.12 to 375 square miles in Virginia and Maryland (Appalachian, Piedmont, Coastal Plain). "A very uniform relation between stream length and drainage area exists in all the streams studied, such that length (measured from a locality ... to the source along the longest channel above the locality) increases directly as the 0.6 power of the drainage area. This rate of increase is not affected, except locally and for short distances, by the geology of the basin." Slope: channel slope proportional to 0.6 power of (median grain size / drainage area); the text gives no separate numeric slope-area exponent. Hack's L ~ A^0.6 is the number to quote. Method: field measurements of more than 100 localities, scatter diagrams (no DEM). Assumption: humid temperate, passive/old landscape; the paper is about a graded, non-tectonic setting.

Gabet et al. 2021 (ESSOAr preprint "Hilltop curvature increases with the square root of erosion rate") challenges the linear assumption; NOT yet read.

- Kirby & Whipple 2012, review, search abstract only; full text not read.
- Mudd, Clubb, Gailleton, Hurst 2018, "How concave are river channels?", ESurf 6, 505-523, doi 10.5194/esurf-6-505-2018, https://esurf.copernicus.org/articles/6/505/2018/esurf-6-505-2018.pdf (read). Key statements:
  - S = ks A^-theta; theta is "concavity index" (text, sec 1). Reference concavity chosen by regression of log S on log A; ksn depends on theta_ref.
  - "the correct concavity index is therefore lower than that indicated by the log[S]-log[A] data" where uplift is higher in the core of a range (their numerical sim, sec 3). Also Kirby and Whipple 2001: downstream uplift decrease in Siwalik Hills (India/Nepal) gave elevated apparent concavities (citing Kirby & Whipple 2001).
  - Field example: Loess Plateau basin, SRTM 30 m DEM from OpenTopography; best-fit theta about 0.45 (sec 3-4 figure 9; peak of likelihood at theta approximately 0.45).
  - Assumption: uniform uplift and steady state; violated in transient landscapes. Method disagreement: slope-area vs chi (Perron and Royden 2013) vs MLE tributary method (Mudd et al. 2014) can differ.
  - Supports: ks positively correlated with erosion rate (Ouimet 2009; DiBiase 2010; Scherler 2014; Mandal 2015; Harel 2016), cited at intro.
- Struble and Roering, "Hilltop curvature as a proxy for erosion rate: Wavelets enable rapid computation and reveal systematic underestimation", ESurf Discussions preprint esurf-2021-40 (35 pp), https://esurf.copernicus.org/preprints/esurf-2021-40/esurf-2021-40.pdf (read in part; preprint, final version not checked).
  - Lidar DEMs at about 1 m grid spacing; smoothing scales lambda = 5 to 141 m. Finding: both the polynomial (PFT, Hurst et al. 2012 style) and wavelet (CWT) hilltop curvature "systematically underestimate CHT at moderate to high erosion rates", approximating a square-root relation with erosion rate (abstract lines 65-73 and 170-172; consistent with Gabet et al. 2021 as they state).
  - Synthetic test: CHT underestimated by more than 20% for E* = 30 hillslopes and by 60% for E* = 100 slopes when lambda = 15 m (sec. results, line 577 of text).
  - Introduction states hilltop curvature is nonlinear at high erosion rates while slope becomes insensitive to erosion rate (Hurst et al. 2012, 2013 cited) (line ~114). Reported erosion rates from the OCR (Oregon Coast Range) catchments 0.007 to 0.178 mm/yr (lines 514-516).
  - DISAGREEMENT with the linear hilltop-curvature model of Hurst et al. (2012): Gabet et al. 2021 (JGR-ES 126, e2020JF005858; ESSOAr 10.1002/essoar.10504389.2, search metadata) and this paper both report square-root dependence.

- Liu et al. 2021, "Analysis of Hillslope Erosion Based on Excess Topography in Southeastern Tibet", Frontiers in Earth Science, doi 10.3389/feart.2021.684365, https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/earth-science/articles/10.3389/feart.2021.684365/pdf (read; 8 pp). Data: 30 m SRTM; 82% of slopes in study area 30 +/- 5 degrees; threshold hillslope taken as 30 degrees; 4,430 landslides, 71% in areas of excess topography above 30 deg; linear correlation (r = 0.83, n = 62 erosion rates) between excess topography volume and mean basin erosion rate; excess topography above 30, 35, 40, 45 deg computed. Catchment slope mode values reported as 32, 33, 26 deg and 29, 29, 32 deg for listed rivers (lines garbled in extraction; treat as approximate).
  - Assumption: threshold slope is set by landslides; the authors state it is a "reasonable range", not a physically derived constant.

- Hypsometry in an old belt: Greater Khingan Mountains, NE China, Frontiers in Earth Science 2021, doi 10.3389/feart.2021.796610, https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/feart.2021.796610 (read in part; 15 pp). 90 m SRTM; HI range 0.13-0.44, mean 0.30; most curves concave; authors say the range is "approaching the old-age development stage, consistent with moderately stable tectonic activity". ### Regional search-level findings (abstract or snippet only, NOT full text)
- Himalaya, Brozovic, Burbank, Meigs 1997, Science 276:571-574, doi 10.1126/science.276.5312.571 (search metadata; Caltech repository record). ### Methods caveats gathered so far
- Hypsometry (search, Strahler 1952 origin): convex high HI = young, concave low HI = old; a Taiwan steady state study reported HI near 0.5 (Chen et al. 2012 Geomorphology 171:1, search); mature and old stages can give identical curves unless monadnocks present (search snippet, researchclopedia). Assumed: HI depends on lithology too.

### More primary reads (open access)
- Grieve, Mudd, Hurst, Milodowski 2016, "A nondimensional framework for exploring the relief structure of landscapes", ESurf 4:309-325, doi 10.5194/esurf-4-309-2016, https://esurf.copernicus.org/articles/4/309/2016/esurf-4-309-2016.pdf (read, 6 pp).
  - Sites: Oregon Coast Range, Gabilan Mesa, Cascade Ridge (northern Sierra Nevada), Coweeta Hydrologic Lab (southern Appalachians, NC). All NCALM lidar gridded to 1 m DEMs.
  - Critical gradient Sc (dimensionless gradient, tan of angle): Oregon Coast Range Sc = 1.2 (Roering et al. 1999 value, as cited); Grieve et al. 2016 use Sc = 0.79 for one site (text lines ~386-400); Cascade Ridge Sc = 0.72 (Grieve et al. 2016b estimate); Hurst et al. 2012 used Sc = 0.8 for Cascade Ridge. The paper says Sc = 1.2 "is too large" for the Oregon site (disagreement with Roering 1999).
  - Converted for this note (my arithmetic, not in the paper): Sc 0.72 = 35.8 deg; Sc 0.8 = 38.7 deg; Sc 1.0 = 45 deg; Sc 1.2 = 50.2 deg.
  - Results: Cascade Ridge shows an erosional gradient; southern Appalachians (Coweeta) read as a landscape decaying after Miocene uplift (abstract). Relief from moving window of radius equal to characteristic hillslope length (Roering et al. 2007 method).
  - Assumption: planar hillslopes near Sc; steady-state curve; sediment flux law nonlinear.
- Stutenbecker, Costa, Schlunegger 2016, "Lithological control on the landscape form of the upper Rhone Basin, Central Swiss Alps", ESurf 4:253-272, doi 10.5194/esurf-4-253-2016, https://esurf.copernicus.org/articles/4/253/2016/esurf-4-253-2016.pdf (open; fetched; abstract read). Abstract: topography controlled by uplift vs erosion; lithology claimed to control landform, which is hard to disentangle from climate/tectonics. Numbers not yet extracted.
- Mudd 2018 and Grieve 2016 together: Mudd's concavity and Grieve's Sc both matter for interpreting "steepness" vs "relief".
- Swiss Alps numbers from Stutenbecker et al. 2016 Table 1 (ESurf 4:253, read; my summary of the 50-row table, my computation): catchment mean slope 19.6 to 40.7 deg (median 30.3); local relief 471 to 991 m (median 736) (relief within 1 km radius per text); hypsometric integral 0.28 to 0.70 (median 0.45); DEM 2 m swissALTI3D, catchments above 10 km2 analysed with 100 m hypsometry bins. Catchment size 10 to 674 km2. Method: DEM slope, local relief, HI, ks and theta from river profiles. These are the only tabulated slope/relief/HI set for a young-ish glaciated Alpine belt I have read so far.
